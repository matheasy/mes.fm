One possible cause could be the loss of thermal tiles…

## Solution: Review of Mechanics of Materials

Note that I will be using Grok AI's assistance in the solution, as well as Dr. Judy Wood's solution key: https://grok.com/share/c2hhcmQtMg_4766b37c-cfd8-4dea-897f-42a2e51d1568

Let's first cover some of the mechanics of materials terms first.

### Strain and Thermal Expansion

We are given the thermal strain for the support plates and aluminum tube, via their coefficients of thermal expansion:

- α<sub>plate</sub> = 0.00 με/°F
- α<sub>al</sub> = 12.8 με/°F

[Strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_(mechanics)) is the relative deformation compared to a reference position, and has [SI base units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_base_units) of meter per meter (m/m).

α is the coefficient of linear [thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion) (tendency of matter to increase in size with increasing temperature).

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23xedu6WzsBwfcPdgiaVHrNUoY3SvvnwaKpW48Aw4DfJWYEFsYqubu84oxGURT45LWFwB.png)
>[Figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion#/media/File:Dehnungsfuge.jpg): [Expansion joint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_joint) in a [road bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_bridge) used to avoid damage from thermal expansion.
	
The units με/°F = microstrain per degree Fahrenheit = 10<sup>-6</sup> strain per °F.

10<sup>-6</sup> strain corresponds to 1 unit of length changed per 1,000,000 unit of length, aka 1 part per million.

For example, aluminum expands by 12.8 millionths of an inch per inch of length for every 1 °F temperature increase.

The plates are assumed to not expand at all with temperature, hence α<sub>plate</sub> = 0.

### 2014-T6 Aluminum Alloy

[2014 aluminium alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_aluminium_alloy) (or aluminum in USA / Canada) is an aluminum-based alloy often used in the aerospace industry.

The T6 refers to the [tempering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempering_(metallurgy)) process used, which is a process of heat treating used to increase the toughness of (typically) iron-based alloys.

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23uaaWn7igjEaGeuybahjYHD4JWWpYAnp4xHG9H3rm76RLg3FDCGJmfh5JUp51mQrMB28.png)
	
>[Figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempering_(metallurgy)#/media/File:Tempering_colors_in_steel.jpg): Differentially tempered steel. The various colors produced indicate the temperature the steel was heated to. Light straw indicates 204 °C (399 °F) and light blue indicates 337 °C (639 °F).[1][2]

The [T6 temper](https://www.makeitfrom.com/material-properties/2014-T6-Aluminum) involves solution heat-treating and artificially aging until the metal meets standard mechanical property requirements.

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23tbPiZnnf37BjJfGkrZM2bkkc5ffyr5HPAFLdMe73CZCJkaL8oqXmJuL1mgg3nRrgtMM.png)

### Elastic Constants

We are given the elastic constants for the support plates and aluminum tube:

- E<sub>plate</sub> = ∞, **G** = ∞, **v** = 0 (rigid)
- E<sub>al</sub> = 10.6 Msi, G = 3.9 Msi, v = 0.34

[Young's modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%27s_modulus) (or the Young modulus), E, is a measure of the tensile or compressive stiffness when a force is applied lengthwise. 

Young's modulus is given by the equation of [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(mechanics)) (force per unit area) divided by the strain.

The [Shear Modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_modulus) or modulus of rigidity, G or sometimes S or μ, is a measure of the [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_(physics)) shear stiffness of a material and is defined (hence the := sign) as the ratio of [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) to [shear strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_strain).

[Shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress), τ, is the component of [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(physics)) [coplanar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coplanar) (on the same plane) with a [material cross section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_cross_section). It arises from the [shear force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_force), the component of the [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) vector parallel to the material cross section. <u>[Normal stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_stress)</u>, on the other hand, arises from the force vector component perpendicular to the material cross section on which it acts.

The resulting shear stress, τ, deforms the cuboid's rectangular side into a [parallelogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram). The area involved would be the top of the parallelopiped.

### Thermal Expansion Properties of the Support Plates and Aluminum Tube
	
In summary, the support plates are considered idealized as perfectly rigid and non-expanding, hence having a coefficient of thermal expansion of zero, an infinite Young's modulus, infinite shear modulus, and a Poisson's ratio of zero.

While the aluminum tube has a coefficient of thermal expansion of 12.8 με/°F, a Young's modulus of 10.6 Msi (million psi aka million pounds per square inch), shear modulus of 3.9 Msi, and Poisson's ratio of 0.34 (the transverse strain is 34% of axial strain).

### Strength Limits (Allowable Stresses)

We are also given the following strength limits for the support plates and the aluminum tube, at which the material is considered to fail (either yield or ultimate strength, depending on the design criteria):

Plates:

- τ<sub>max</sub> = 10.2 ksi = maximum allowable shear stress
- σ<sub>max</sub> = 104 ksi (tension) = maximum allowable normal stress in tension
- σm<sub>max</sub> = 70 ksi (compression) = maximum allowable normal stress in compression

Aluminum: 

- τ<sub>max</sub> = 19 ksi
- σ<sub>max</sub> = 60 ksi (tension)
- σ<sub>max</sub> = 60 ksi (compression)

Note that ksi = kips per square inch = 1000 pounds per square inch = 1000 psi

## Solution to Part (a): Maximum temperature without failure

>Determine the maximum temperature of the aluminum tube without causing failure at point A, B, or C. 
>	
>Note: Failure may occur in the plate or the tube.

Let's look at locations A, B, and C in the earlier given diagrams, also shown below.

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/48Pxf1tvTYEP3JFnckfmJkcRFWEap7M5zxSvtWuMsprJcGY3T5EqCi2exQKaxjXs6T.png)

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23uFuVRWVNCpS5szvg7riSYTfrghG7wMBNwNChrzKm15W87YLTm6wFS4Mb5NhwV52unKX.png)
	
We are given that the one aluminum tube is exposed and heated uniformly while the top and bottom support plates (and the rest of the structure) stay at 0 °F.

At 0 °F the aluminum tube is stress-free and there are no gaps.

The plates are modeled as rigid (E = ∞, G = ∞, v = 0, α = 0), so the length L of the tube is fixed.

Only the exposed tube wants to expand due to thermal expansion; the rigid plates prevent that expansion, producing compressive force in the tube and equal-and-opposite reactions on the plates.

Failure can occur in the aluminum tube or in the plates (bearing or shear).

Because L is constant:

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23tHbg1MP4ByMPrjdkJkDFyz7FDLtZNXySwdLQeoVy1q1SZWDwe9s7ycWomGU121KS6Q4.png)

### Candidate 1: Aluminum tube in compression (point C)

If the first failure is aluminum in compression, then the maximum temperature is:

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/Eo6SAAzW3kpoCfMTUefdTEZyudfjh8nGEXySUPW5bYg2x7Ku5dNSCUZ2Fd3gqVzD6dS.png)
	
[Calculation check](https://mes.fm/calculator?q=60000%2F(10.6*12.8)):

>60,000/(10.6*12.8) = 442.217 
	
### Candidate 2: Plate bearing at the tube end (point B)

The support plate's compressive allowable stress is given as σ<sub>max</sub> = 70 ksi.

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23zGwRkHd6UWLdHMB9CNATf9ZwtTdTVp2EEjH2i5BojBYMVq7MoZka8VdFjtjkrRSAyCa.png)

If the point at B fails, then the compressive stress is greater than 70 ksi.

But we have already checked the point at C, which has a compressive strength of 60 ksi in the aluminum tube, thus the aluminum controls as it would fail before the plate does.

Since the support plates are idealized as rigid and non-expanding, we can't just plug in α = 0 and E = ∞ into the earlier ΔT formula to obtain the temperature at this higher stress.

As the aluminum tube expands due to thermal expansion, this expansion imposes stress on the support plate as well.

Thus, we can obtain the maximum temperature that the support plate will fail at by simply scaling up our earlier maximum temperature by the ratio of the compressive strength of the support plate to the aluminum tube.

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23viTTuZXQXQNL56k66EUFS7Hj8VhyDu5P4h6iVAQYFTN8hLQEgaS4RVdWaxA21R7LNQr.png)

Note again that the aluminum tube at point C will fail before this temperature is reached at point B in the support plate.

[Calculation check](https://mes.fm/calculator?q=70%2F60*442.217):

>70/60 * 442.217 = 515.9198 
	
### Candidate 3: Shear at the plate connection (point A)

First note that we are told to assume that the "remaining structure is completely insulated from the heat and remains at 0 °F".

The skin of the wing, aka the support plate, is not affected by the temperature, but it is affected by the thermally expanding aluminum tube.

This thermal expansion of the aluminum tube causes shear stress on the support plate, at the point A.

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/EocDFcDJgDFyLvzGmo3xpGJrfRG8otXNbwSrBhbefSSuoP4sTqHSYa6V8Co3KBFf69Y.png)

Thus, by equilibrium:

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23tmmf7kj9j3K89tJKbDLu5LkNAs5igDRVCY8MFK9nCfpDuEBTXpgoTf8376fqG81yuP7.png)

Recall that the maximum allowable compressive stress in the aluminum is 60 ksi, while the maximum allowable shear stress in the support plate is 10.2 ksi.

Thus we can now obtain the corresponding temperature increase of the aluminum tube that would cause the support plate to fail in shear. 

![image.png](https://files.peakd.com/file/peakd-hive/mes/23twA2Y16xvTDMQ28Sht1NXepASfVXg51mUKnxXovawUevuymmMYvwkKaEdq27Gu24Gdi.png)

[Calculation check](https://mes.fm/calculator?q=10200%2F(10.6*12.8)*(0.3%2F0.11)):

>10,200/(10.6*12.8)*(0.3/0.11) = 205.0279 

This is the lowest temperature rise!

Therefore the maximum allowable temperature of the tube is 205 °F.

## Summary of the Engineering Logic

Because the plates are rigid, the aluminum tube develops compressive stress linearly with temperature.

The connection, however, can transmit only a limited shear force before the plate fails in shear.

That force limit is reached at only 205 °F, which is well before the aluminum itself reaches its 60 ksi compressive yield.

Hence the first failure is shear at the plate-to-tube interface (point A).

>**Space Shuttle**

>The Space Shuttle was a partially reusable spacecraft operated by the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA).[2]: 5, 195  It flew in space for the first time in April 1981,[3]: III-24  and was used to conduct in-orbit research,[3]: III-188  and deploy commercial, military, and scientific payloads.[3]: III-66, 68, 148

>**Here is a factual list of the documented controversies, management failures, known risks, and missed opportunities related to the Space Shuttle Columbia (STS-107) disaster.** 
	
>These are drawn primarily from the official Columbia Accident Investigation Board (CAIB) report, contemporary news reporting, and released internal NASA emails and documents. The physical cause was a foam strike during ascent that breached the left-wing leading-edge Reinforced Carbon-Carbon (RCC) panels; superheated plasma entered during re-entry. The CAIB concluded that NASA’s organizational culture was as much a cause as the foam itself.
>	
>Known Foam-Shedding Problem and Normalization of Deviance
>	
>- Foam loss from the External Tank occurred on more than 80% of missions for which imagery was available. It was a chronic, known issue.
>- NASA had repeatedly treated foam strikes as a maintenance/turnaround problem rather than a potential safety-of-flight risk. Requirements that debris strikes not damage the thermal protection system were routinely waived.
>- A significant foam strike on Atlantis (STS-112) just months earlier was classified as an “action” item rather than a more serious in-flight anomaly that would have required resolution before the next flight. Schedule pressure (including ISS construction goals) contributed to this decision.
>- The CAIB described this as classic “normalization of deviance”: each successful flight with foam strikes was taken as evidence that the risk was acceptable.
>	
>**Imagery Requests for Damage Assessment Were Not Obtained**
>
>- During the mission, multiple engineers (including Rodney Rocha of the Debris Assessment Team) **requested higher-resolution imagery of the left wing** using Department of Defense assets (spy satellites or ground telescopes).
>- Rocha wrote that without better images it would be “very difficult to even bound the problem” and asked colleagues whether they could **“petition (beg)”** for outside agency assistance.
>- Requests were blocked or not acted upon. Mission Management Team chair Linda Ham’s actions and communications contributed to the cancellation of at least one request. Associate Administrator William Readdy later stated he declined to make an “emergency” request for satellite imagery after engineers had already concluded there was no safety-of-flight issue, though he said non-interfering imaging could proceed (it did not).
>- The CAIB identified this as one of several missed opportunities. Managers viewed the requests as non-critical engineering desires rather than operational needs. The crew and senior decision-makers never received definitive visual confirmation of the damage extent.
>- Post-accident, NASA arranged for routine military imaging of orbiting shuttles.
>	
>**Internal Engineering Concerns and Incomplete Communication**
>
>- Engineers (including Robert Daugherty at Langley and others) exchanged emails discussing **possible catastrophic scenarios from the foam strike**, including damage near the wheel well, tire failure, or structural breach leading to loss of vehicle and crew. Some used language such as “world of hurt” or described worst-case outcomes.
>- These concerns largely remained at the mid-level engineering level. They were not effectively escalated into a formal safety-of-flight challenge that altered the Mission Management Team’s conclusions.
>- A Boeing analysis (using a tool primarily calibrated for smaller tile impacts) concluded the strike was not a safety concern. Management accepted this analysis. The actual damage was to the RCC leading edge, outside the primary assumptions of that tool.
>- **The crew was informed** via email that there was **“absolutely no concern”** for RCC or tile damage and that the issue was **“not even worth mentioning”** except to avoid being surprised by reporter questions. They were **not told of the ongoing internal debate** among some engineers.
>
>**Organizational and Cultural Factors (CAIB Findings)**
>
>- The CAIB stated that “the NASA organizational culture had as much to do with this accident as the foam.”
>- Key issues included: inverted burden of proof (prove the strike was unsafe rather than prove it was safe), communication barriers and informal processes that diluted urgency, schedule pressure, resource constraints, and a history of treating the Shuttle as operational rather than developmental.
>- Similar cultural issues had been identified after Challenger; the CAIB noted that lessons had atrophied.
>- There were at least eight identified missed opportunities during the flight to better understand the damage.
>	
>**Media and Public Perception Notes**
>	
>- Landing-day weather at Kennedy Space Center was excellent (clear conditions after morning fog burned off). Contemporary accounts described it as near-perfect weather for the scheduled touchdown. Re-entries were not normally major news events, but STS-107 received elevated attention in part because it carried the first Israeli astronaut (Ilan Ramon) and a large suite of science experiments.
>- I did not locate a specific major newspaper headline with the exact phrasing “A perfect day for a perfect mission.” Coverage commonly described the mission as appearing successful and the weather as favorable until contact was lost.
>	
>**What Was Not the Case**
>
>- There is no credible evidence in the official investigation or contemporaneous reporting of deliberate sabotage, intentional withholding of a known fatal breach, or a decision to fly despite confirmed knowledge that the vehicle would be lost. The core failures were cultural, analytical, and communicative: a known foam risk was underestimated, damage-assessment tools were insufficient for the actual strike, and requests for better data were not pursued vigorously enough under the belief that the strike was not a safety-of-flight issue and that little could be done in any case.
>
>The CAIB report (especially Volume I) remains the primary authoritative source for these points. Many of the internal emails were released via FOIA or during the investigation and are publicly available.
