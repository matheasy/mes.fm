Claude AI Questions

Questions

  1. How did the idea start in 2017, and who was the "team of researchers" the website mentions? What did the 10+ years of development involve?
  2. Why anchor Day 1 to the Winter Solstice, and not the spring equinox (the old Roman / Persian new year) or perihelion (about January 3)?
  3. The solar year is about 365.2422 days, not 365. Where does the leap day go, and does the calendar stay "perpetual" (same weekday for every date) in leap years?
  4. The solstice falls between December 20 and 22 and on different dates in different time zones. Which time zone decides Day 1?
  5. The NPR story says 9-day weeks. 73 = 8 × 9 + 1, so what happens to the 73rd day of each season? And why are the days named after planets ("a Saturn Day")?
  6. The season names differ across the site: Winter / Spring / Summer / Autumn / Fall on the About page, but Winter / Sprinter / Summid / Fautumn / Midsea in the Time Converter. Which names are final?
  7. Are the five seasons about sunlight (astronomical) or temperature (weather)? Summer is centred on the June solstice, but the hottest weeks come 4 to 6 weeks later in most places.
  8. How does it work in the Southern Hemisphere, the tropics (wet / dry seasons) and near the poles?
  9. How would everyday life convert: birthdays, holidays, pay days, school terms, and business quarters (5 seasons vs 4 quarters)?
  10. How does it compare with past reform attempts: the French Republican calendar (10-day weeks), the International Fixed Calendar (13 months of 28 days), the World Calendar and the Hanke-Henry calendar? Why did those fail, and what makes this one different?
  11. Has anyone actually adopted it (schools, farms, companies, apps)? How do people use it alongside the Gregorian dates?

Critiques

  1. 365 = 5 × 73 is neat, but a leap year has 366 days = 2 × 3 × 61, so the five equal seasons only work in 3 years out of 4 unless the leap day sits outside the seasons.
  2. "The only pair of factors" leaves out 1 × 365, and the year length is a measurement, not a whole number, so factoring 365 can sound more like number play than science.
  3. Calling the fifth season a "discovery" may be a stretch: the seasons are a choice of how to split the year, not something you can measure.
  4. "Perpetual" (days and dates never shift) can't hold with a 7-day week, and even with 9-day weeks the extra 73rd day and leap days need special rules.
  5. The Gregorian calendar already keeps the solstices and equinoxes within about a day or two, so is the gain mostly in equal season lengths and naming, not accuracy?
