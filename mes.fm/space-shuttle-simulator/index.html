Coming home starts half a world away. The Shuttle turned tail-first and fired its two OMS engines for about 2 to 3 minutes, slowing by roughly 90 m/s (300 ft/s) out of about 7,700 m/s. That small burn drops the far side of the orbit into the air. About half an hour later it hits the entry interface (120 km), then glides some 7,000 km more to the runway.

How the entry is modelled: a Kepler orbit down to 120 km, then a lifting glide over a round Earth (US Standard Atmosphere layers, lift/drag about 1 above Mach 8 rising to 1.5, ballistic coefficient from the mass, a 250 m² wing and a drag coefficient of 0.84). Like the Shuttle's guidance, the bank angle is chosen every half second so the deceleration follows a planned drag profile; the steepness slider scales it. The heating number is the Sutton-Graves estimate for a 0.8 m nose. It stops at 760 m/s (about 25 km up), where the Shuttle's final approach began, and adds that last 96 km. It is a teaching model, not the real guidance.

What the Shuttle would have needed

Earth's gravity only (no pull from the Moon, so no figure-eight free return). Real missions also aim at where the Moon will be when they arrive, about 3 days later for Apollo.

What this simulator does

It flies a Space Shuttle around a real-sized Earth with real gravity: every orbit is a true Kepler ellipse in metres and seconds, the Earth turns under it at the right speed, the Sun lights the right half, and (if you leave it on) Earth's equatorial bulge slowly turns the orbit the way it turns the International Space Station's by about 5° a day. You plan burns the way flight controllers did: pick a moment on the orbit, choose how much to push forward, sideways or up, and see the new orbit before you commit.

Missions

Raise the orbit
A Hohmann transfer: one burn forward makes the far side of the orbit higher, a second burn half an orbit later makes the new orbit round.
Tilt the orbit
A push sideways (normal) at the equator turns the orbit's plane. It is expensive: one degree at 7.7 km/s costs 134 m/s, almost half the Shuttle's propellant. That is why launches aim for the right plane from the start.
Catch the station
The famous puzzle of orbital rendezvous: to catch up with something ahead of you, you drop lower (lower orbits are faster), and to slow down you speed up. The station view shows the Shuttle in the station's frame, with the V-bar (along the orbit) and the R-bar (up and down).
De-orbit and land
Pick a runway and the planner finds the orbit pass and burn time that bring the glide down near it, about 90 m/s of braking. Then watch the entry: the plasma glow, the g-force and the ground path to the runway.

Questions

How fast did the Space Shuttle go?
About 7.7 km/s (27,700 km/h, 17,500 mph) in a typical 300 to 400 km orbit, going round the Earth every 90 to 92 minutes.
How big was the de-orbit burn?
Usually about 90 m/s (around 300 ft/s), a 2 to 3 minute firing of both OMS engines, done about an hour before landing on the opposite side of the world.
What is the entry interface?
The point 400,000 ft (about 120 km) up where the air starts to matter. The Shuttle crossed it at about 7.6 to 7.8 km/s, around 1.2° below horizontal, nose up at 40°.
Why didn't the Shuttle go to the Moon?
It could not carry enough propellant: about 300 m/s of speed change against the 3,100 m/s needed just to leave Earth orbit, and it had no heat shield for the faster return. See the Moon tab for the numbers.
How accurate is this?
The orbits, burns, periods and plane changes are exact two-body physics with J2 drift. Air drag is only used for the entry, the Moon's and Sun's pull are left out, and the entry glide is a teaching model of the Shuttle's guidance, so treat landing points as rough (hundreds of km).
