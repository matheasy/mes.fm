Why does the wave leak? Inside a barrier that is higher than the particle’s energy, the Schrödinger equation has no wavy solution. Instead the wave dies away smoothly, like e−κx, with κ = √(2m(V₀−E))/ℏ. It never jumps straight to zero, so if the barrier is thin some of the wave is still there on the far side, and from there it travels on as an ordinary wave. The chance of getting through falls off roughly like e−2κa for a barrier of width a: double the width and the chance is squared.

A ball would bounce back 100%. Classically a particle with less energy than the barrier is always reflected, and a particle with more energy always gets over. Quantum mechanically both rules are broken: waves get through barriers that are too high, and part of a wave reflects even from a barrier it could climb (try “Above the barrier”).

The packet is not split into two particles. |ψ|² gives the probability of where you would find the particle if you looked. After the collision there are two lumps: if you put a detector on the far side, it clicks with probability T and the particle is then entirely there.

How the simulation works (and what is simplified)

The wave is stored on a grid of 1,024 to 4,096 points and moved forward in tiny time steps with the Crank-Nicolson method: each step solves (1 + iHΔt/2ℏ)ψnew = (1 − iHΔt/2ℏ)ψold, a set of equations with only three diagonals that is solved in one sweep. The step is exactly unitary, so no probability is lost except at the two edges (outside the picture), where an absorbing layer quietly removes the wave, as if it flew off to infinity, and counts what it removed on each side. That is how “Reflected” and “Transmitted” keep counting after the lumps leave the picture. (The popular split-operator Fourier method was tried first: at a sudden jump in V its splitting error sprays a little of the wave into very high momenta, which is invisible for big transmissions but doubled tiny ones such as the STM gap.)

The chart below the simulation is computed separately and exactly, with the transfer-matrix method (matching ψ and ψ′ at every edge of a piecewise-constant potential; smooth shapes are cut into 600 thin slices). Because a packet contains a spread of energies, the fair comparison is the exact T averaged over that spread (the ring); the simulated dot lands on it when the run is over.

Units: energies in electronvolts (eV), times in femtoseconds (1 fs = 10−15 s). For an electron, lengths are in nanometres. For a heavier particle the same picture holds with every length shrunk by √(mₑ/m), so a proton needs a barrier about 43 times thinner to tunnel as much. The “alpha decay” preset is the Gamow picture drawn at electron scale; real alpha decay has MeV energies and femtometre distances. This is a one-dimensional model: real barriers are three-dimensional, but the leaking works the same way.

Why only certain energies? A wave squeezed between walls must fit: in a box of width L only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit, so the momentum and therefore the energy come in steps, En = n²h²/(8mL²). Smaller box, bigger steps; heavier particle, smaller steps. That is why atoms (tiny boxes for light electrons) have sharp spectral lines, while a marble in a jar seems to have any energy at all.

The shape of the well sets the spacing. Infinite well: gaps grow like n². Harmonic oscillator (a spring): perfectly even steps of ℏω, starting at ½ℏω, never at zero (the zero-point energy). Morse (a real chemical bond): steps that shrink until the molecule falls apart. Finite well: only a few levels fit below the rim, and the waves poke out into the walls a little: that is tunnelling again.

Why does a mix move? Each level’s wave turns in time at its own rate, e−iEnt/ℏ. On its own that turning is invisible (|ψ|² stays put: a “stationary state”). Two levels together beat against each other, and the probability sloshes with period h/ΔE. In the double well the two lowest levels are almost equal, so the beat is slow: the particle sits in one well, then tunnels to the other and back, every h/ΔE. The ammonia maser works on exactly this flip-flop of the nitrogen atom.

How the levels are found

The well is cut into 600 to 1,200 grid points and the Schrödinger equation becomes a large matrix whose only non-zero entries are on three diagonals (the second derivative becomes (ψi−1 − 2ψi + ψi+1)/Δx²). Its eigenvalues are the energies. They are found one by one by Sturm-sequence bisection (counting how many eigenvalues lie below a trial energy, and halving the interval 40-odd times), and each wave by inverse iteration. The test suite checks the results against the exact answers: n² for the box, (n+½)ℏω for the spring, Airy-function zeros for the bouncer, the Morse formula, and the number of levels that fit in a finite well. Hard walls are placed at the ends of the picture; for wells that do not hold every level (finite, Morse), only the truly bound levels are shown.

The H₂ preset uses the usual Morse parameters for hydrogen (well depth about 4.75 eV, width parameter about 1.94 Å⁻¹, bond length 0.741 Å) and the reduced mass of two protons. The model gives a first vibrational gap near 0.52 eV, close to the measured 0.516 eV, and 17 bound levels; real H₂ has 15 (v = 0 to 14), because a real bond is not exactly a Morse curve.

Wave picture

What you are seeing. The wave goes through both slits; behind the wall the two parts overlap, and where crest meets crest the wave is strong, where crest meets trough it cancels. Each particle is still detected at one single spot. Where it lands is random, but the chance of each spot follows the wave’s brightness, so the stripes appear only after many particles.

Dark stripes are where the paths from the two slits differ by half a wavelength: d sinθ = (m + ½)λ. The whole pattern sits inside the hump of a single slit, whose first zero is at a sinθ = λ.

Pilot-wave paths (the de Broglie–Bohm interpretation) draw one possible path for each particle, steered by the wave with velocity v = (ℏ/m) Im(∇ψ/ψ). Starting points are drawn at random from |ψ|², and the paths bunch into the bright stripes. Standard quantum mechanics predicts the same dots without saying what path the particle took; interpretations differ on that question, not on the measured pattern.

Honest scaling. Real experiments have slits tens of thousands of wavelengths apart, so the stripes are very close together. The wave picture keeps the real shape (the ratio d : a and the number of slits, so the same number of stripes per hump) but draws the wavelength much bigger so you can see it. Why it happens. To make a short, sharp bump you have to add up waves of many different wavelengths, and wavelength is momentum (p = h/λ). A long, smooth wave needs only a few. So a packet that is well located (small Δx) has a wide spread of momenta (large Δp). This is a property of waves, not of clumsy measuring.

Let it go. A free packet keeps its momentum spread, so its fast parts run ahead of its slow parts and it spreads out. For any packet whose shape starts out real, Δx(t)² = Δx₀² + (Δp t/m)²; for the Gaussian, Δp = ℏ/(2Δx₀), which gives the formula below. The narrower you make it, the faster it spreads.

\( \displaystyle \Delta x\,\Delta p \;\ge\; \frac{\hbar}{2}, \qquad \Delta x(t) = \Delta x_0\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\,\Delta x_0^{2}}\right)^{2}} \)

Why 2 is the limit for hidden variables. Suppose each pair leaves the source carrying instructions: “if your dial is at a, say +1; at a′, say −1”, and so on, and that Alice’s answer cannot depend on Bob’s dial (locality). Then for each single pair the four products A(a)B(b) − A(a)B(b′) + A(a′)B(b) + A(a′)B(b′) = A(a)[B(b) − B(b′)] + A(a′)[B(b) + B(b′)]. One bracket is 0 and the other is ±2, so every pair contributes exactly +2 or −2, and the average can never be bigger than 2. Bell’s 1964 model (each pair carries a random hidden angle; each side answers + if its dial is within 45° of it for photons, 90° for spins) reaches exactly 2 at the best angles, which is as well as any local model can do there. Quantum mechanics reaches 2√2.

What this simulation is. The quantum source draws each pair’s two results together from the joint probabilities above, with a seeded random number generator. That is the honest way to reproduce quantum predictions on one computer; it is not a model of how nature does it. The two hidden-variable sources really are local: Alice’s answer is computed from her own dial and the shared hidden angle only. Detectors here are perfect and every pair is counted, which real experiments had to work hard to approach (see the loopholes below).

Loopholes, one line each

  • Locality loophole: if the settings are chosen too slowly, a light-speed signal could tell one side what the other picked. Fix: choose settings at random after the particles leave, far enough apart.
  • Detection (fair-sampling) loophole: if many particles go undetected, the counted ones might be a biased sample. Fix: detect a large enough fraction of all pairs.
  • Freedom-of-choice loophole: the “random” settings might be correlated with the hidden variables. Narrowed by using quantum random generators, distant starlight and even human choices.
  • Memory loophole: hidden variables could use earlier trials; handled by statistics that don’t assume independent trials.

What does it mean? (interpretations)

The experiments settle that no theory with local hidden variables and freely chosen settings matches nature. They do not say which assumption to give up, and physicists still disagree:

Non-locality (e.g. de Broglie-Bohm pilot-wave theory, collapse theories): the measurement on one side really does affect the other, but in a way that cannot be used to send messages. Copenhagen-style views: there were no definite values before measurement; the quantum state is a tool for predicting results. Many worlds: every outcome happens, and the correlation shows up when the branches of Alice and Bob meet. Superdeterminism: the settings were never independent of the particles, so the inequality’s assumption fails. Each fits all the data here; the simulator only shows the predictions.

An amplitude is the number whose square is the probability. For spin ½ turned by an angle θ from the beam’s own spin direction the amplitudes are cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2), so the chances are cos²(θ/2) and sin²(θ/2). A beam from the hot oven has no single spin direction (a 50/50 mixture), so it has no amplitudes, only probabilities.

\( \displaystyle |{+}\theta\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|{\uparrow}\rangle + \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|{\downarrow}\rangle \)
\( \displaystyle P({+}\theta \mid {\uparrow}) = \cos^2\tfrac{\theta}{2} \)
Spin ½: a magnet turned by θ from the spin passes cos²(θ/2) of the atoms to its + output. At 90° (Z then X) that is ½; at 180° it is 0.
\( \displaystyle d^{1}(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1+\cos\theta}{2} & -\frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt2} & \frac{1-\cos\theta}{2} \\ \frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt2} & \cos\theta & -\frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt2} \\ \frac{1-\cos\theta}{2} & \frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt2} & \frac{1+\cos\theta}{2} \end{pmatrix} \)
Spin 1 (Wigner d-matrix): from +1 along Z, a magnet at 90° gives ¼, ½, ¼ into its +1, 0 and −1 beams.

Z then Z: atoms that came out “up” from the first magnet all come out up again. The answer is repeatable.

Z then X: a magnet turned 90° splits them 50/50. Knowing the spin along Z tells you nothing about X.

Z, X, Z: keep only the up-along-X atoms, then measure Z again: half come out down, although every one of them was up along Z two magnets earlier. Measuring X wiped out the Z information.

Recombined: if the X magnet’s two beams are brought back together with nothing recording which one an atom took (a careful “Stern-Gerlach interferometer”, very hard in practice), the original spin comes back intact and the last magnet sends every atom up. Put a detector on the paths (“watched”) and the 50/50 split returns: it is the which-path record, not the magnet, that destroys the information.

What is drawn. The magnets are drawn side on, splitting beams up and down whatever their angle, with the angle shown on each dial; the passing beam is re-centred before the next magnet. The spot sizes and spacing are schematic. The probabilities are exact quantum mechanics; each atom’s path is picked at random from them.

The 1922 experiment

Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms from an oven through an uneven magnetic field in Frankfurt in 1922 and collected them on a glass plate. Classical physics expected the atoms’ magnets to point every which way and paint a continuous band. Instead the deposit split into two separate parts. At the time it was read as proof of Bohr and Sommerfeld’s “space quantization”; the real reason, the electron’s spin (a silver atom’s magnetism comes from one unpaired electron), was only proposed by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit in 1925. Chains of magnets like the ones above are the classic thought experiments used to teach spin (Feynman, Sakurai); with spin-½ atoms the two spots are the whole story.

Bohr’s model and the quantum cloud. Bohr (1913) pictured the electron on circular orbits of radius n²a0 and got these energies exactly right, which is why the line colours match. A quantum mechanics lab in your browser

MES Quantum Lab puts the famous experiments of quantum mechanics side by side, each one solved live from the real equations rather than played back as a video. Change a number and the wave, the counts and the graphs change with it. Nothing is uploaded: everything runs on your own computer or phone.

What is here

  • Tunnelling: a wave packet runs into a barrier it does not have the energy to climb, and part of it still comes out the other side. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is solved step by step, and the result is checked against the exact transmission formula.
  • Wells and energy levels: why a trapped particle can only have certain energies, the ladder of levels for boxes, springs and double wells, and a particle tunnelling back and forth between two wells.
  • Double slit: particles arrive one at a time as single dots, yet the dots build up stripes. Turn on a which-path detector and the stripes disappear. Pilot-wave (de Broglie-Bohm) paths show one way of picturing it.
  • Uncertainty: squeeze a wave packet in position and its spread in momentum grows: Δx·Δp is never less than ħ/2.
  • Entanglement and the Bell test: “spooky action at a distance”. Measure entangled pairs at angles you choose and see the CHSH number go above the limit of 2 that any local hidden-variable model must obey, while each side alone still sees pure 50/50 noise, so no message travels faster than light.
  • Stern-Gerlach: silver atoms split into two spots, not a smear, and a chain of magnets shows how measuring one direction wipes out what was known about another.
  • Hydrogen atom: the real orbitals (1s, 2p, 3d, 4f and more) as clouds and 3D shapes, the energy levels and the coloured lines of the hydrogen spectrum.
  • Calculators: de Broglie wavelength, photon energy, the uncertainty principle, tunnelling probability, particle in a box, the Bohr model, Compton scattering and blackbody radiation.

Questions

Is quantum tunnelling real?

Yes. It is measured every day: it is how a scanning tunnelling microscope sees single atoms, how flash memory stores bits, why alpha particles escape from nuclei, and part of why the Sun can fuse hydrogen at its temperature.

Does entanglement let you send messages faster than light?

No. The correlations between the two sides are stronger than any classical explanation allows, but each side on its own sees random results. The pattern only appears when the two lists are brought together, which needs an ordinary signal. The Entanglement tab shows this with live counts.

What does the double slit prove?

That single particles such as electrons, neutrons and even large molecules arrive as dots but are distributed like a wave going through both slits. What that means is the subject of the interpretations of quantum mechanics (Copenhagen, pilot wave, many worlds and others), which all predict the same pattern.

Are these simulations exact?

They use the standard equations of non-relativistic quantum mechanics, solved numerically on a grid (the wave tabs) or exactly (the Bell test, Stern-Gerlach, hydrogen orbitals and calculators). Each tab says what is simplified, such as working in one or two dimensions or in scaled units.
