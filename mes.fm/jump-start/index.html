How to Jump-Start a Car: Interactive Simulator, Battery Age and Voltage Check

Free interactive guide to jump-starting a car: drag the jumper cable clamps in the right order on a simulated engine bay and see what goes wrong if you don't, use a jump pack, push-start a manual, decode your battery's date code (like F-15), see how cold cuts cranking power, and check the battery's voltage.

Safety first. This page teaches the usual method. Your car’s owner’s manual wins if it says something different (some cars have special jump points or forbid jump-starting). If a battery is cracked, leaking, swollen or frozen, do not jump it.

    Push-starting (bump-starting) a manual car

    Only for a car with a manual gearbox. An automatic, a hybrid or an electric car cannot be push-started, and many modern cars with keyless start or a start-stop system will not run with a very flat battery even if you get them rolling.

    1. Clear road ahead, ideally a gentle downhill slope, and one or two helpers to push. Never push on a busy road.
    2. Driver: ignition on (dash lights on), clutch pedal fully down, select 2nd gear (1st is too jerky).
    3. Helpers push until the car rolls at a brisk walk to a jog, about 8–16 km/h (5–10 mph).
    4. Release the clutch quickly. The wheels turn the engine; it should fire. Press the clutch again straight away and give it a little throttle so it doesn’t stall.
    5. Keep it running: drive for at least 30 minutes, then have the battery and alternator tested.

    If it doesn’t fire after two or three tries, stop: repeated misfires pump unburnt fuel into the exhaust, which is hard on the catalytic converter. Never tow-start a car with an automatic gearbox.

    How old is my battery?

    Most batteries carry a date code: a sticker on the side or top, a code heat-stamped into the plastic lid, or a shop’s “sold” sticker. A very common format is a letter for the month and a number for the year: A = January, B = February … so F-15 on a sticker or stamp is usually June 2015. Formats differ by maker, so the decoder lists every likely reading.

    “Sold” punch stickers have the months and years printed round the edge; the shop punches out the month and year it was sold. That is the sale date, not the build date: a battery can sit on a shelf for months, so a battery more than about 6 months old at sale is no longer fresh. A one-digit year repeats every ten years, which is why the decoder shows the most recent match. If two readings are possible, the sticker on the side and the stamp on the lid usually agree.

    Why batteries die (and why it happens on the coldest morning)

    ☀️20 °C
    Mild

    Battery makers’ rule of thumb (a fully charged battery): at 0 °C (32 °F) it can deliver about 65% of its warm-weather cranking power and at −18 °C (0 °F) about 40%, while thick cold oil means the engine needs about 1.55× and 2.1× as much to turn over. Outside −18 to 27 °C the lines are extended and only a rough guide.

    ⏳ Age

    Most car batteries last 3 to 5 years. The lead plates slowly sulfate and shed, so the battery holds less and less charge. You often don’t notice until the first cold morning.

    🥶 Cold

    The chemistry slows down and the oil thickens (see the slider). Cold reveals a weak battery; it rarely kills a healthy one by itself. A flat battery can also freeze.

    🔥 Heat

    Summer heat is the real killer: it speeds up corrosion and water loss inside. In hot climates batteries often last only 2–3 years. The damage is done in summer and shows up in winter.

    🛣 Short trips

    Starting uses a lot of charge and a 5-minute drive does not put it back. Lots of short trips (or a car that sits) leaves the battery part-charged, which sulfates it.

    💡 Lights or doors left on

    Headlights draw about 10 A: they can flatten a battery in a few hours. A dome light or a glovebox light that stays on can do it overnight.

    🔌 Parasitic drain

    Clocks, alarms and computers draw a little all the time (usually 20–50 mA). A faulty module or aftermarket gadget can draw 10 times that. Try the calculator below.

    🧂 Loose or corroded terminals

    White or green crust on the posts blocks current. Clean with a baking-soda solution and a brush (eye protection on), then tighten.

    ⚙️ Charging problem

    If a new battery keeps going flat, the alternator or its belt may not be charging. The running-voltage check below tells you.

    Voltage check: is my battery charged?

    A cheap multimeter on the 20 V DC range, red probe on +, black on −. For the resting reading, the engine should have been off for a few hours (or switch the headlights on for a minute, then off, to remove the “surface charge” after driving).

    Resting voltage (ordinary battery, ~25 °C)ChargeWhat to do
    12.6 V or moreabout 100%Fine
    12.4 Vabout 75%OK; a long drive or a charger tops it up
    12.2 Vabout 50%Charge it soon: below this a battery sulfates and can freeze in a hard frost
    12.0 Vabout 25%Weak; may not start in the cold
    11.9 V or lessflatCharge it, then have it load-tested

    Engine running, the alternator should show about 13.7–14.7 V. Under 13 V it is probably not charging; over about 15 V it is overcharging. Many newer cars with “smart” charging let the voltage drop to 12.5–13 V for a while when the battery is full, so check twice. AGM batteries rest about 0.1–0.2 V higher than ordinary ones.

    CCA and the other numbers on the label

    CCA (cold cranking amps)The current a 12 V battery can deliver for 30 seconds at −18 °C (0 °F) while staying above 7.2 V. The number that matters for winter starts: pick at least what your manual asks for.
    CA / MCA (cranking amps)The same test at 0 °C (32 °F), so always a bigger number than CCA. Don’t compare one battery’s CA with another’s CCA.
    RC (reserve capacity)Minutes the battery can supply 25 A (at 27 °C) before dropping to 10.5 V: how long you could run with a dead alternator.
    Ah (amp-hours)Total charge, usually at a 20-hour rate. Used for the drain calculator above.
    Group sizeCase size and post layout (for example 24F, 35, H6 / 48). It must fit the tray and the cables.

    🧵 Cable gauge

    Lower number = thicker wire. 6 gauge is fine for small cars, 4 gauge for most cars and SUVs, 2 or 1 gauge for trucks, V8s and diesels. 4–6 m (12–20 ft) long lets you reach without parking nose to nose. Thin cheap cables get hot and may not start a big engine.

    ⚡ Hybrids and electric cars

    They still have a small 12 V battery that wakes the car up. Use the 12 V battery or the jump points under the hood shown in the manual, never the orange high-voltage parts. Many makers say not to use a hybrid or EV to jump another car (its small 12 V system and converter are not built for a starter motor): check the manual first.

    🧊 Frozen battery

    A flat battery can freeze at about −8 °C (a full one only near −58 °C). Bulging sides or ice in the cells means never jump or charge it: it can burst. Let it thaw indoors, away from people, then test it, or replace it.

    🛑 Cracked, leaking, swollen

    Don’t jump it. The acid burns skin and eyes and eats paint. A rotten-egg smell or a hissing battery means stop and step away.

    🔄 AGM and start-stop cars

    Usually jumped the normal way, but many have a battery sensor on the − post: clamp to the ground point the manual shows, not the post. Some have the battery in the trunk with jump posts under the hood. Replacement AGM batteries may need to be “registered” to the car.

    💻 Protect the electronics

    Turn off headlights, heater fan, radio and chargers in both cars before connecting. Don’t rev the donor hard. Don’t disconnect while cranking.

    🥽 Safety gear

    Safety glasses and gloves; no smoking, flames or sparks near the batteries; remove metal watches and rings; keep loose clothing and hair away from fans and belts (an electric fan can start by itself).

    📞 Call roadside assistance when…

    the battery is damaged or frozen, you can’t find the jump points, it’s a hybrid / EV you are unsure about, the car is on a busy road or shoulder, it won’t start after 3–4 tries, or anything smokes, smells or melts.

    Printable checklist: jumper cables

    How to jump-start a car

    Connect the cables in this order: red to the dead battery’s +, red to the good battery’s +, black to the good battery’s −, and the last black clamp to bare metal on the dead car’s engine. Start the good car, wait a few minutes, start the dead car, then remove the clamps in the reverse order.

    Questions

    Why not put the last black clamp on the dead battery’s − post?
    The last connection closes the circuit and can make a small spark. Lead-acid batteries can give off hydrogen gas from their vents, more so while being charged, and hydrogen ignites easily. Making that spark on engine metal away from the battery keeps it away from the gas. Some owner’s manuals do allow − to −; follow yours.
    Does the order really matter?
    Yes. Connecting the dead car’s + first means the only live clamp you handle early is the one you are about to clamp; and grounding last puts the unavoidable spark in the safest place. Removing in reverse order keeps a live red clamp from being waved around near metal.
    What happens if I swap the clamps?
    Reversed polarity sends a large current the wrong way. Expect sparks and hot or melted cables, blown fuses or fusible links, and possibly damage to the alternator’s diodes and the car’s computers. In the worst case a battery can burst. If you notice a mistake, remove the clamps (the last one first) and check fuses before trying again.
    How long should I let the donor car run?
    Two to five minutes before the first try. A very flat battery may need longer. Crank for no more than about 5–10 seconds at a time, with a minute or two between tries, so the starter doesn’t overheat.
    It started. Now what?
    Keep it running and drive for at least 30 minutes (motorway driving is best) so the alternator can recharge it. A jump-start doesn’t fix an old battery: if it was the battery’s age, cold or a drain, have it tested. Many auto-parts shops test batteries for free.
    It clicks but doesn’t crank.
    Fast clicking means not enough current: check that every clamp bites bare metal, let the donor run longer, or use thicker cables. A single click with full dash lights can be the starter motor.
    How do I read the date on my battery?
    Use the decoder above. A letter + number like F-15 is most often month + year: F is the 6th letter, so June 2015. Formats vary, so compare the sticker and the lid stamp, or ask the maker.
