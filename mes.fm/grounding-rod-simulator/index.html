What a ground rod does, and what it doesn't

  • It ties your wiring to the earth's potential, so the whole system can't float to a dangerous voltage compared with the ground you stand on (a nearby lightning strike, a utility line touching a primary line, static build-up).
  • It does not make breakers trip. Even a perfect rod is in series with the utility's own earth, so a hot-to-case fault through earth is only a few amps. Tab 2 shows this. The thing that trips the breaker is the grounding wire (EGC) and the neutral bond at the panel, which give the fault a metal road back to the transformer.
  • It cannot "absorb" a lightning strike. A strike carries tens of thousands of amps; the rod's job is to be one part of a bonded system so everything rises together. Tab 3.
  • It does little for carpet shocks. Static is drained by humidity and surfaces long before the house earth matters. Tab 4.

GFCI / RCD: the part that protects you

A GFCI (US/Canada, trips at about 5 mA) or RCD (elsewhere, usually 30 mA) compares the current going out with the current coming back and cuts power within ~25 to 40 ms when a few mA go astray, whatever the grounding. What actually protects a home

  • Bonding, not draining. Every rod, water pipe, gas pipe, antenna mast, dish and the electrical ground must be tied together. Then everything rises and falls as one and nothing sparks between them. Two rods that aren't bonded can differ by hundreds of kilovolts in a strike (the third number above).
  • Surge protectors at the panel (many places now require one) and at sensitive gear clamp the surge on the power lines; the rod gives them a place to send it.
  • A lightning protection system (air terminals and down conductors) is a separate trade; its ground must be bonded to the electrical ground.
  • Standing outside is the danger, not the house: when thunder is within 30 s, go indoors and stay off corded phones, plumbing and wired electronics.

How grounding handles static

  • The time constant is R × C. A person is only ~100 to 200 pF, so even a 1 GΩ path drains them in a fraction of a second; a good insulator (R of 1013 Ω or more) holds charge for hours.
  • The earth's own resistance is irrelevant. A rod at 5 Ω or at 500 Ω makes no difference next to the resistance of the carpet, shoe or hose in the path. That is why anti-static work uses wrist straps and floors of about 106 to 109 Ω to ground (fast, but not a shock hazard) and bonding wires between a fuel container and the filler, not extra rods.
  • Humidity keeps a thin film of water on surfaces that drains charge: dry winter air is why you get zapped.
  • Where gas or dust can ignite (fuel, grain, solvents, oxygen) bond and ground everything and keep sparks below the table's energy.

Home electrical safety checklist

  • Press TEST / RESET on every GFCI or RCD each month; replace any that won't trip.
  • Look for 3-prong outlets that aren't actually grounded (an inexpensive outlet tester shows open ground and reversed wires).
  • Check that the water and gas pipes are bonded to the panel's ground (an electrician can see the clamp and wire).
  • Have a whole-house surge protector fitted at the panel and plug sensitive gear into a rated surge strip.
  • Keep any antenna, dish, cable and phone entrance grounded to the same system, not to a separate rod.
  • Don't remove the ground pin or use cheater adapters; don't use a "ground rod" as a substitute for a ground wire.
  • Have the grounding electrode connection, rod clamps and corrosion checked when the panel is upgraded; measure rod resistance if your code or a TT supply requires it.

Ground rods, earthing and electrical safety explained

A ground rod (earth electrode) is a metal rod driven into the soil and wired to your electrical panel. Its resistance to earth is set mostly by the soil, then by the rod's length and the number of rods. This tool calculates it, shows what a rod does and does not do in a shock fault, models the voltages in the ground around a lightning strike, checks static discharge time, and summarises the main rules by region.

Common questions

  • How deep should a ground rod be? In the US an 8 ft (2.4 m) rod fully in contact with the soil; in Canada rods are typically 3 m (10 ft); in Australia at least 1.2 m. Longer rods help more than fatter ones because the current reaches deeper, often wetter soil.
  • Why two ground rods? In the US, one rod must be supplemented by another electrode unless it measures 25 Ω or less, and rods must be at least 6 ft apart so they don't share the same patch of soil. Compare 1 and 2 rods in tab 1.
  • Does the ground rod trip the breaker? No. Tab 2 shows the fault current through a rod is only a few amps; the grounding wire to the panel and the neutral-ground bond clear faults.
  • Does a ground rod protect from lightning? It is one part of a bonded system. It does not conduct a strike harmlessly; tab 3 shows the voltages.
  • What resistance should a ground rod have? It depends on the system and country: 25 Ω is the US benchmark for a single rod; TT systems use RA × IΔn ≤ 50 V; many practical designs aim for much less. Tab 5 lists the main rules.
