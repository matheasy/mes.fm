YouTube Audio Analyzer: Loudness (LUFS), Waveform and Spectrogram

Free YouTube audio analyzer: measure any video's loudness in LUFS, true peak and loudness range, and see its waveform, spectrogram, silences and clipping. Works with YouTube, 3Speak, Rumble, Odysee, BitChute, X and your own audio or video files. Nothing is uploaded.

Everything is measured in this browser. Nothing you play, drop or record is uploaded.

        How this works

        A web page is not allowed to read the sound inside a YouTube player (or any other site's player): the browser keeps other sites' media sealed off. There are three honest ways around that, and this page uses all of them:

        1. A file you drop. The exact sound, measured from start to end in a few seconds. Video files work too (MP4, MOV, MKV, AVI…). For your own YouTube videos, download the file from YouTube Studio.
        2. A direct link to an audio or video file (ending in .mp3, .mp4, .wav…), when the site holding it lets other pages read it. If it doesn't, download it and drop it.
        3. Listening to a tab. For YouTube, 3Speak, Rumble, Odysee, BitChute, X and Substack, the video plays in the site's own player and the browser shares that tab's sound with this page (you choose the tab, and it asks first). It measures in real time, so a 10-minute video takes 10 minutes; you can stop any time.

        What the numbers mean

        Integrated loudness (LUFS)
        How loud the whole thing feels, as one number (ITU-R BS.1770, the method YouTube, Spotify and TV use). YouTube plays everything at about −14 LUFS: louder videos are turned down, quieter ones are left quiet.
        True peak (dBTP)
        The highest point of the sound wave, including the peaks that happen between samples. Keep it at −1 dBTP or lower so it doesn't distort after it is converted to AAC or Opus.
        Loudness range (LU)
        How much the loudness changes between the quieter and louder parts (EBU Tech 3342). Speech is often 3 to 8 LU; a film can be 15 or more.
        Momentary and short-term
        Loudness over the last 0.4 s and the last 3 s, the two lines on the loudness chart.
        Peak-to-loudness ratio (PLR)
        True peak minus integrated loudness: how much punch is left. Very low values (under 8) mean heavily squashed audio.
        Noise floor
        The level of the quietest tenth of the recording, usually room noise or hiss between words.
        Clipping
        Places where three or more samples in a row sit at full scale: the wave was cut flat and you hear distortion.

        Questions

        Why does a YouTube video measure quieter than my file?

        Because YouTube turned it down. Its player lowers loud videos to about −14 LUFS before you hear them, and this page hears what the player plays. Stats for nerds shows how much (content loudness); type that number in the box under the results to get the upload's own loudness. Also check that the player's volume slider is at 100%.

        Is the tab's sound recorded?

        No. The page keeps only the measurements (loudness every 0.1 s, the waveform's outline and the spectrogram), never the sound itself, and none of it leaves your browser. Sharing stops when you press Stop or close the tab.

        Why can't it just download the YouTube video?

        YouTube does not offer that to other websites, and getting around its protections is against its terms. Playing the video in YouTube's own player and listening to the tab is allowed and needs nothing extra.

        Which loudness should I aim for?

        For YouTube, about −14 LUFS with true peaks at or below −1 dBTP. For podcasts, −16 LUFS. Broadcast TV: −23 LUFS (Europe) or −24 LUFS (North America). Our Audio Editor can set the loudness of a file for you.
