Dr. Judy Wood and MES: the Space Shuttle Columbia Emails

Emails between Dr. Judy Wood and Mathiew Estepho (MES), September 19 to October 31, 2026, oldest first

These are the emails between Dr. Judy Wood and me (Mathiew Estepho, MES) about the Space Shuttle Columbia disaster and the EM 304 test problem she gave her students at Clemson University on February 6, 2003, five days after Columbia was lost. They are in the order they were sent, from September 19 to October 31, 2026 (times are Pacific time).

Two months after the test, NASA came up with an updated location of the failure. (Now it matched mine. When the final report came out several months later, there was a student doing a work study at an engineering company. He called me that day and said he'd kept his test paper and ran around his work place showing it off. (He had the right answer.)

I think I told you that my nickname was "The Disaster Queen.

I picked the failure location based on where it looked like the foam hit the wing. The fact that the foam made a right-angle turn when it hit told me that it hit hard. You couldn't see exactly where it was because it was on the other side of the wing from the camera (under the wing), but it dustified when it hit. Looks like I picked it correctly. NASA didn't have it correct for the first two months until they found the equivalent of the flight-data-recorder. That thermocouple showed that's where the failure was.

The Shuttle was destroyed on Feb. 1, 2003. This test was given Feb. 6, 2003.
You can see by the detailed drawings that this event really bothered me. They knew the foam damaged the wing. A care package was sent up to the space station Feb. 2, a day later. Why not just keep the shuttle in orbit and re-aim the care package to go to the shuttle. (Care package = oxygen, food, supplies....) And then they could send up another care package for the space station a few days later.

This was the standard cover sheet (except for the bottom).
I didn't start the clock until everybody understood the problem. I assumed it would be scary for them because it was a "real" problem and not a cartoon problem. Part of what was being tested on this test was to draw stresses on a cube, bearing stress, shear stress, and compressive stress, and thermal expansion.

Answer for the lowest failure temperature, 205 deg. F
Note that the failure temperature from the thermocouple reading was about 200-210 deg F on the NASA chart.

:-)

It showed the students that simple calculations can get you pretty close. :-)

G. Scott Hubbard is an experimentalist, like I am. So when the panel met, I watched from his perspective.
I think he had this answer, himself, from the start because of something Harold Gehman said (the leader of the investigation panel).
When they did the final test (shooting foam at a real wing) and it made the exact same damage, Scott Hubbard called up Gehman to tell him the results of the test. Gehman said to Hubbard, "I beg you're grinning ear to ear right now." That's why I think Hubbard had it figured out early on. But the panel went trough all sorts of fault trees. It was an excellent panel!

Sheila Widnall, whose area of expertise is dynamics. She was fascinated by the 2nd day object that followed the shuttle after it did it's back roll maneuver. Then it started to tumble out of orbit. Based on the way it tumbled, it's orbital decay rate, etc., she figured out what size and shape it was. It was the missing chunk that was punched out of the wing. When they did the roll maneuver, that chunk fell out of the wing.

With Hubbard's final test, shooting foam at a real wing, it punched out the same size chunk of wing and it went into the wing. It would have fallen out with a roll maneuver.

I've only included the shuttle problem. I don't know where the other three problems are. But this is the fun one. :-)

This was for a Mechanics of Materials class, not Statics.
Statics is the prerequisite.
Some call it Strength of Materials.
This is the fist test of the semester, so it's not much past Statics. But the Statics part is quite simple.
It's understanding the formulas for shear stress and normal stress (compressive stress and bearing stress).
The biggest deal is to know what area to put in the formula. ;-)

For this, I showed Roadrummer cartoons in class. That would help them figure out what area mattered.
Like when Roadrunner puts birdseed on a bridge, then cuts a circular hole around the birdseed when the Roadrunner stops to eat. The shear stress area is the circumference of the circle times the thickness. Some idiots just throw in the surface area and guess, hoping for only a few points off. But I told them when I showed the cartoon to remember what surface was holding up that disk of road.. ;-)

The visual of the Roadrummer cartoons really fixed it in their minds. Aother one was the Coyote being an arrow and launching himself. He rams into a cactus. Figure out what area is keeping him from flying through.

These were examples for shear stress.

OH!!!! you may have liked my corny joke in Statics class. It was introducing moments and moment arms. I came in with a big telescoping sea fishing rod. I had a fishing lure on the end, that was a fish filled with buckshot to exactly 4 ounces I'd show him to the class and say this guy here (the silver fish lure) weighs 1/4 pound. So we'll call him "Mr. QuarterPounder." The I start extending the telesoping rod way out until I'd say, "But way out there, he feels like a whopper!"

Corny, I know. But it's something that will stick in their minds.
Before that, and on the second class, we had multiple tug of wars. They were 3-way. On each group were 3-4 roles. A force has a point of contact, a magnitude and a direction. There was a metal ring, and 3 rops extending out. One of the roes had a spring scale. The objective was to calulate the force in the other two ropes. Because this was Statics class, we needed the forces to be constant. So the role of the 4th kid was "Newton Police." He had a big protractor to measure the angle between each rope, requiring equilibrium to measure it (thust Newton Plolice). Either he would write down the angles and the force in one rope, or that was the job of the fifth guy. Then they'd all sit down and figure out how to calculate the other two forces.

I had a few of these activities through the semester. Later on, I had mini teeter-totters, like what used to be at playgrounds. This was desk size. I had a bos of nuts and bolts and otherr heavy objects. They were to place several nuts along one side, then one big one on the other side. Tht is, the sum of the moments on one side = the moment on the other side. There were scales on each side of the mini teeter totter.

Actually, that came to mind when realizing the tipping top of WTC2 appeared to stop tipping. I thought of the little teeter totter with a big weight on one side and a bunch of little nuts distributed along the other side.

When we did trusses, I hd a box of tong depressors, wooden sticks, with a hoe in each end. Make a square, then see how it flops into a parallelogram. At a cross piece, and it beomes rigid. I had severall of these and pased them arond in the class.

Some of them were like I was. It isn't so much what you learn from the thing in your hand, it's about having something to play with in your hand that seemed to make the brain work better. ;-)
So I often brought in "show and tell" items.... just to pass aroud. It made class less boring. ;-) It helps the student feel like he's learning something.

OK, I'll save more stories for later. I bought all the tug-of-war ropes so still have them. ;-)

I had a Mechanics of Materials book, but it looked scary, like a highly technical book written in the 1800s.

I ended up making workbooks for both classes. They had notes, equations, blank areas to take notes, and example problems. One side was a blank sheet with the FBD on it. The other side had the solution. So you could work out a problem, or sit and watch me work it out while you looked at the solution. (Left side was the un-worked side, right side was the solved side, so both were in view). At the end of each class was a "group work problem." The idea was to help each other start working on a problem and give your new information a test drive. You could get help from each other or me ...whatever you needed. If you just turn the students loose after lecturing, they don't start their homework nutil the night before the next class and they've forgotten stuff by then. So trying out stuff at the end of class helped them get started. For motivatioin, theey were guaranteed to have a "groupwork problem" on the next test. I would change the numbers and/or needing to solve for something else in the problem. That was bait to motivate them to work on the problem instead of leaving class early.
Golly, I miss those classes.

My solution is a bit messy. I do the solutions while the students are working on the test. Then, while grating, if I find that students had their solution in a different set up, I'll have that in there, too.
You'll see the "price list" for how many points for each part.

That keeps my grading very consistent.

I give 4 problems. Some easier and some harder. So I make each one worth 25 points.
That's my way of normalizing the scores. That is, if a hard problem is worth more points, there would be high scores and low scores with not much in between.

Memories

I taught Statics on MWF, and EM304 was T,H, 2 and 3 days after the Shuttle came apart.

Two days and three days after the Shuttle came apart, I walked into my class and said, "Most people my age and older can remember where they were when they learned that JFK was shot." I pointed to the approximate seat where I was in first grade. Instead of using the intercom/speaker in each room, the principal came to our door and talked with our teacher. She burst into tears. I told my 2003 class that I didn't know who this JFK dude was, but something bad awful had to have happened to make our teacher cry like that.
Then I told them that they would remember this day (2003) when their teacher was really upset and angry. I explained that I had wanted to be an astronaut and cold relate to those people. The ground crew KNEW of the impending disaster and DID NOT RELAY THE SERIOUSNESS to the crew, They had the crew download all of their data before the initiation of descent. The crew was seen as expendable.

I think I first said that the cause was the same as the blowing up of the Challenger. That is, politicians making engineering decisions.

When I explained that the foam punched a hole in the wing, destroying the protective tiles, a few students said, "The shuttles lose tiles all the time." I abruptly said, NO, not before or during re-entry. Maybe when they touchdown on the runway on earth, but they must have those tiles through re-entry. A student wondered why. I said because of thermal expansion, if nothing else. Thermal expansion can tear something apart. Consider a can of pop in the freezer. Why does it explode?
Then I said, "Hey, you guys have just learned about thermal expansion. You have a test on Thursday. Maybe I'll put this problem on your test. ;-)

This is how it began.

The Shuttle problem is fairly easy. It's just about knowing what equations to use and what numbers to plug them into.
I really wanted the students to learn how "back of the envelope" calculations (as such approximations are called)) are very useful.
How did they do?
About 1/2 got it 100% correct.
Another 1/3 kinda got it.
And about 1/3 didn't even try. "If NASA can't figure it out, how do you expect us to?" <<== This is the biggest problem.

The ones that kinda got it right, typically hadn't paid close attention to the Roadrunner cartoons. That is, for calculating shear, it's a different surface area than the normal stress.
There were a few who came up and asked me. (I help them recall memories. I don't form new memories.) I asked if they remember the Roadrunner cartoons. Bingo. The student now "got it," ;-)

When I showed them the Roadrunner cartoons, I said that lazy students often jjust throw some area numbers into the equation. They don't know which area is correct, but assume they will get "partial credit." I told them NOPE. After watching these cartoons, you should know what area to use.

No, I was not the typical teacher. ;-) I really wanted the students to learn the material. I also wanted it to be kinda fun.

At Vs. Tech, I taught the equivalent of EM304 testing lab. Clemson didn't have a lab with EM304, but should have.
At Va. Tech, we called it "Busting Lab." We measured the failure strength of materials and how they broke. To add excitement, I'd place a 1-cent or 5-cent bet on the failure load. The winner was the closest without going over. Not required to bet on it, but it made it fun. You know they remembered how things turned out, too.
There were other tricks I'd pull. Cast iron would break with a bang. First test ductile steel.. There was a safety plexiglass around the specimen. So when we were testing Cast Iron and students would get a bit close, when it went BANG! --you can bet every student remembered that. ;-)

Examples I gave for what you'd want a ductile failure or brittle failure with.... Re-bar in bridges..... they start sagging and you know to schedule bridgework the next summer. For brittle failure, I used the example of bolts holding on airplane engines. If the bold is to fail, you want that engine gone,. You don't want it hanging at some weird twisted angle and moving around. That came to mind with the recent disasters of engine dropping off a plane on take-off. But there's no way to deal with that. But if it happened shortly after takeoff, or higher, it would have been ok.

OK, so much for remembering the good times.

Oh, ya.... In Statics class, on the first day, I explain that "everything comes down to tug-of-wars or teeter-totters." I call it a teeter-totter. I think others call it a see-saw. In any case, the kids now (at least in the USA) don't learn what teeter-totters are. They are outlawed. Same with merry-go-rounds and monkey bars. And playgrounds are filled with rubber chunks, not pavement. God forbid that someone would get a skinned knee..... ;-)

Normal young kids bounce. They don't shatter. It's the best time for them to learn about the world.
