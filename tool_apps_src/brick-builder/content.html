How is the strength worked out?

Every part's weight (and any weight you add) flows down through the parts under it to the baseplate. Two things can go wrong:

  • Overhang: when the weight carried by a part sits past the edge of what it rests on, the studs have to pull to stop it tipping. Each stud grips with about 1.5 newtons; more studs further from the edge hold more.
  • Bending: a part that spans a gap bends like a beam. Parts stacked on top over the gap bend with it, so a deck three plates thick is far stiffer than one plate. If it sags more than 1/40 of the span, the studs pop off.

Smooth tiles and the sloped faces have no studs, so nothing on them is held. It is a simple model for learning, not an engineering tool: real bricks also stretch, twist and wear out.

The building steps are the order the parts were added. Step through an example to build it with real bricks.

How does it decide what breaks?

When the front hits, every part has to be stopped in a few thousandths of a second. That takes a force (its mass times how fast it slows down), and the force has to reach the wall through the bricks in front of it or through stud joints.

  • Pushing on the brick in front is free: plastic does not break by being squeezed.
  • Each stud joint holds about 1.5 newtons sideways (less for old, loose bricks). If a joint has to hold more, it lets go and everything on the far side flies off.
  • Pieces that fly off can break again when they hit the road or the wall.

So a car built from tall stacks held by one or two studs smashes at low speed, while one built from overlapping plates and bricks survives. Faster crashes and harder walls (concrete stops it fastest, foam slowest) break more. A simple model for fun and learning: real bricks also crack and bend.
