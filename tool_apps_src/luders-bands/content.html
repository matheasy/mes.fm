Pull a bar of mild steel and, just after it starts to yield, a dull stripe appears at one end and creeps along the bar at a slant while the force stays flat. That stripe is a Lüders band. Press Start and watch one form, then add a notch, change the testing machine, or bake the bar and test it again.

Unload, wait, pull again: strain ageing

Stretch the bar past the plateau, take the load off, and pull it again. Straight away it just carries on hardening: the dislocations are free. Wait (or bake it) and the carbon atoms find them again: the yield point and a new Lüders band come back, higher than before.

Inside the metal: why there is an upper yield point

Click anywhere on the bar above to watch that slice. Not to scale: a real dislocation line is about a millionth of a millimetre across, and millions of them break free together in each grain the front crosses.

Why the band leans at about 55°

Draw a circle on the sheet, then stretch it: it becomes an ellipse, longer along the pull and narrower across. Only two directions keep their length, where the circle and the ellipse cross. The band front has to lie along one of them, because the stretched steel behind the front and the unstretched steel ahead of it must stay joined along the whole front without tearing or overlapping.

Drag the orange handle (or the slider) to turn a line on the sheet and read how much it stretches. r is the Lankford value: how much a sheet thins in width compared with thickness. r = 1 is an evenly behaving metal and gives 54.7°; deep-drawing steels have r ≈ 1.6 to 2, so their bands lean a little less.
