Pull a bar of mild steel and, just after it starts to yield, a dull stripe appears at one end and creeps along the bar at a slant while the force stays flat. That stripe is a Lüders band. Press Start and watch one form, then add a notch, change the testing machine, or bake the bar and test it again.

Words explained
Lüders band
A stripe of steel that has yielded (stretched for good) while the steel next to it has not stretched at all. It starts at one end and grows along the bar.
Band front
The edge of the band: the thin seam between stretched and unstretched steel. It is the only place the metal is moving during the plateau. It runs along the bar at a slant (about 55°).
Stress
How hard the bar is being pulled, per square millimetre of its cross-section: force ÷ area, in MPa (newtons per mm²). A bigger bar needs more force for the same stress.
Strain
How much longer the bar has got, as a fraction of its starting length. 1% strain on a 50 mm bar = 0.5 mm longer.
Elastic
Springy stretching. Let go and the bar goes back to its old length.
Plastic strain / yielded
Stretching that stays after you let go. A slice that has "yielded" has started stretching for good.
Upper yield point
The peak of force just before the first band forms. The steel holds out a little longer than it should, because carbon atoms pin its dislocations (see Inside the metal).
Lower yield / plateau
The flat part of the force curve while the band crosses the bar. The force hardly changes because all the machine's pulling goes into moving the front.
Lüders strain (εL)
How much a slice stretches the moment the front passes it, usually 1-3% for mild steel. Every slice jumps by about this much.
Time
Seconds of the test (real time, not the playback speed you chose).
Yielded (%)
How much of the gauge length the band has already covered.
Band fronts
How many fronts are moving right now. A band that starts in the middle (a notch) has two fronts, one going each way.
Front speed
How fast a front moves along the bar. Formula: V ÷ (fronts × εL), with V the machine's pulling speed. Two fronts each move half as fast.
Work hardening
After the band has crossed the whole bar, the steel gets stronger as it stretches more, so the force rises again.
Dislocation
A line-shaped flaw in the rows of atoms. Metals stretch by these lines sliding through the crystal, a row at a time.
Strain ageing
Leave stretched steel for weeks (or bake it) and the carbon atoms move back onto the dislocations, so the yield point and the bands come back.
Gauge length
The thin, measured middle part of the test bar (here 50 mm). The thick ends are held in the grips.
Soft / stiff machine
How much the testing machine itself stretches. A soft machine stores spring energy, so the force drop at the upper yield point looks smaller.

Both directions are equally good: the bar does not care. See “Which way does it lean?” further down.

Unload, wait, pull again: strain ageing

Stretch the bar past the plateau, take the load off, and pull it again. Straight away it just carries on hardening: the dislocations are free. Wait (or bake it) and the carbon atoms find them again: the yield point and a new Lüders band come back, higher than before.

What am I looking at? (in plain words)

1. Metal stretches like you move a heavy rug. Dragging the whole rug at once is too hard. Instead you push a ruck (a little wrinkle) across it, and the rug ends up moved by one ruck's width. Metal does the same: rows of atoms slip over each other one small step at a time, not all at once.

2. The ruck in a metal is called a dislocation. Somewhere in the crystal there is one extra half-row of atoms squeezed in (blue, ⊥). Push sideways and it hops along one atom at a time. When it has crossed the whole crystal, the top half has slid one atom over the bottom half. Billions of these hops make the bar longer.

3. Carbon atoms are the doorstop. Under the end of the extra half-row the atoms are spread apart, so there is a roomy spot. Small carbon atoms (orange) love that spot. They moved there long before the test: while the steel cooled at the mill, or over weeks on the shelf. During the test they hardly move at all.

With carbon sitting under it, the dislocation is stuck. It takes extra force to tear it away: that is the upper yield point. Once it is torn free it slides on easily and leaves the carbon behind, so the force drops to the lower yield.

4. Side view vs top view. The side view is a thin slice through the crystal, so the dislocation shows up as one ⊥. In real life it is a long line running into the picture. The top view looks down on that line: carbon atoms hold it at many points, so under stress it bows out between them like a rope held by nails. That is why it looks curvy.

After it breaks free the top view shows many wavy lines: the free dislocations multiply and sweep across one after another. They are wavy because lines are flexible and keep catching on things; when they tangle, the steel gets harder (work hardening).

Why the band leans at about 55°

The short answer: the band front is a seam. Behind it the steel has already stretched; ahead of it the steel has not. Both sides are the same piece of metal, so the seam has to be a line that comes out the same length whether you measure it on the stretched side or the unstretched side. Only a line tilted about 55° from the pull does that.

  1. Pulling makes the bar longer. A line drawn along the pull gets longer.
  2. It also makes the bar narrower (the extra length has to come from somewhere). A line drawn straight across gets shorter.
  3. In between, one tilt does neither. It gains as much from the stretching as it loses from the narrowing, so its length does not change. For ordinary steel that tilt is 54.7°, and the seam can only run that way without tearing or wrinkling.
The maths, and why some sheets lean a bit less

Stretch the bar by a strain ε. Its volume stays the same, so the width and thickness share the shrinking: the width gets narrower by ε/2. A line at angle θ from the pull then changes length by ε (cos²θ − ½ sin²θ). That is zero when tan²θ = 2, so θ = 54.7°.

Some rolled sheets thin more in width than in thickness. The ratio is the r value (Lankford value). The width then shrinks by ε·r/(1 + r) and the band angle is tan²θ = (1 + r) / r. r = 1 gives 54.7°; deep-drawing steels have r ≈ 1.6 to 2, so their bands lean a little less (about 52°).

Step by step: where 54.7° comes from
  1. Name the stretches. Put x along the pull, y across the width and z through the thickness. When the band front passes, the steel there stretches by a strain \( \varepsilon_x \) along the pull (about 2% for mild steel) and gets narrower by \( \varepsilon_y \) and thinner by \( \varepsilon_z \) (both negative).
  2. The volume stays the same. Metal flowing plastically does not get denser or lighter, so the three strains add up to zero:
    \( \displaystyle \varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y + \varepsilon_z = 0 \)
  3. Share the shrinking between width and thickness. The r value (Lankford value) says how they share it: \( r = \varepsilon_y / \varepsilon_z \). Put \( \varepsilon_z = \varepsilon_y / r \) into step 2:
    \( \displaystyle \varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{r}\right) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \varepsilon_y = -\frac{r}{1+r}\,\varepsilon_x \)
    For an ordinary bar r = 1, so \( \varepsilon_y = -\tfrac12\varepsilon_x \): 2% longer means 1% narrower.
  4. Draw a short line at angle θ. A line of length ℓ at angle θ to the pull reaches \( \ell\cos\theta \) along the bar and \( \ell\sin\theta \) across it.
  5. Stretch it. The along part grows by \( (1+\varepsilon_x) \), the across part by \( (1+\varepsilon_y) \). Pythagoras gives the new length:
    \( \displaystyle \ell'^2 = \ell^2\left[\cos^2\theta\,(1+\varepsilon_x)^2 + \sin^2\theta\,(1+\varepsilon_y)^2\right] \)
  6. Keep only the small terms. The strains are a few percent, so their squares are tiny: \( (1+\varepsilon)^2 \approx 1 + 2\varepsilon \) and \( \sqrt{1+2a} \approx 1 + a \). With \( \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 \):
    \( \displaystyle \frac{\ell'}{\ell} \approx 1 + \varepsilon_x\cos^2\theta + \varepsilon_y\sin^2\theta \)
    So the line's own strain is \( \varepsilon_\theta = \varepsilon_x\cos^2\theta + \varepsilon_y\sin^2\theta \). At θ = 0 it is \( \varepsilon_x \) (grows), at θ = 90° it is \( \varepsilon_y \) (shrinks). That is the meter under the picture.
  7. The seam must not change length. On one side of the front the steel has stretched, on the other it has not, and the two sides are joined. A line along the seam belongs to both, so it must come out the same length:
    \( \displaystyle \varepsilon_\theta = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \varepsilon_x\cos^2\theta = -\varepsilon_y\sin^2\theta \)
    (The thickness can still jump across the seam: that small step is part of what makes the band visible.)
  8. Divide by \( \varepsilon_x\cos^2\theta \) and use step 3:
    \( \displaystyle \tan^2\theta = -\frac{\varepsilon_x}{\varepsilon_y} = \frac{1+r}{r} \)
  9. Put in r = 1.
    \( \displaystyle \tan^2\theta = 2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tan\theta = \pm\sqrt2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = \pm 54.74^\circ \)
    The strain itself cancels out: the angle is the same for a 1% band and a 3% band.
  10. Check it with numbers. \( \varepsilon_x = 2\% \), \( \varepsilon_y = -1\% \), θ = 54.74°, so \( \cos^2\theta = \tfrac13 \) and \( \sin^2\theta = \tfrac23 \):
    \( \displaystyle \varepsilon_\theta = 0.02\cdot\tfrac13 - 0.01\cdot\tfrac23 = 0 \;\checkmark \)
  11. Notice the ± in step 9. There are two answers, +54.7° (leaning ╱) and −54.7° (leaning ╲). They are mirror images and equally good. That is why the picture has two dashed lines, and why real bands lean either way.
Which way does it lean?

The maths gives two directions, ╱ and ╲, and a straight, even bar pulled straight has no reason to prefer one. So the choice is made by small things the maths leaves out:

  • Where the first band starts. It almost always starts at a shoulder, where the bar widens into the grip, and at one corner of it, where the stress is a little higher. A start at the top corner and a start at the bottom corner give opposite leans.
  • Slight bending. Grips that are a tiny bit out of line bend the bar while they pull it, so one edge is pulled harder and yields first.
  • Grains and scratches. The grains near the starting point are tilted at random, and a scratch or a burr from machining can start the band.

Once it has started it keeps its lean. The steel just ahead of the front is pushed hardest along the existing seam, so the front runs on the same way. A new band (starting at the other shoulder, or after strain ageing) picks again and can lean the other way. A notch or hole in the middle often starts both at once: two bands crossing in an X, or a V. In wide sheets you see ╱ and ╲ bands side by side.

Try it: in “The bar and the machine”, set Which way the band leans to ╱, ╲ or Take turns, and use the ╱ ╲ buttons on the picture to test the other direction.
