    What is VAT?

    VAT (value added tax) is a consumption tax charged on the value that each business adds as a product or service moves from producer to final customer. Every seller in the chain charges VAT on their sale and deducts the VAT they paid on their own purchases, so in the end the whole tax is paid by the final consumer. More than 170 countries use a VAT or its close cousin GST (goods and services tax); the biggest exception is the United States, which uses a state-level sales tax instead.

    Two prices matter: the net price (also called the price excluding VAT, ex-VAT or exclusive) and the gross price (including VAT, inc-VAT or inclusive). This calculator converts between them, and finds the VAT amount that sits between the two.

    How to calculate VAT

    Add VAT to a net price

    VAT amount = net price × VAT rate
    Gross price = net price × (1 + VAT rate)

    Example: a net price of 100 at 20% VAT. VAT = 100 × 0.20 = 20. Gross = 100 × 1.20 = 120.

    Remove VAT from a gross price

    Net price = gross price ÷ (1 + VAT rate)
    VAT amount = gross price − net price

    Example: a price of 120 that includes 20% VAT. Net = 120 ÷ 1.20 = 100. VAT = 120 − 100 = 20. A common mistake is to take 20% off 120, which gives 96 and is wrong: the VAT is 20% of the net price, which is only 16.67% of the gross price (rate ÷ (100 + rate)).

    Work backwards from the VAT amount

    Net price = VAT amount ÷ VAT rate, and gross = net + VAT. If a receipt shows 20.00 of VAT at 20%, the net price was 100.00 and the total 120.00.

    Find the VAT rate

    VAT rate = (gross − net) ÷ net. A net of 100 and a gross of 107.50 means a 7.5% rate.

    VAT vs. GST vs. sales tax

    • VAT (Europe, UK, much of Asia, Africa and Latin America): charged at every step of the supply chain; businesses reclaim the VAT on what they buy, and the displayed shelf price normally includes VAT.
    • GST (Australia, New Zealand, Canada, India, Singapore ...): the same mechanism as VAT under another name. Canada's provinces blend it into an HST.
    • Sales tax (United States): charged only once, at the final sale to the consumer, and added at the till on top of the shelf price. Rates are set by each state plus local governments, so there is no single national rate. Use the “add VAT” direction of this calculator with your combined state and local rate.

    Which countries have no VAT?

    The best-known exception is the United States, which has no national VAT or GST and relies on state and local sales taxes instead (five states — Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon — have no state sales tax). A small number of other jurisdictions, mostly some Gulf states and tax havens, also have no VAT; check locally. Several countries have unusual set-ups: Malaysia uses a sales and service tax, Brazil a patchwork of federal, state and municipal taxes, and Thailand, Vietnam and Indonesia currently apply special or temporary rates (see the notes beside the rate chips).

    Worked examples

    • UK: a freelancer invoices £850 net. VAT at 20% is £170, so the client pays £1,020.
    • Germany: a shelf price of €59.90 includes 19% VAT. Net = 59.90 ÷ 1.19 = €50.34, VAT = €9.56.
    • Canada, Ontario: a $200 net price carries 13% HST: $26 of tax, $226 in total.
    • Several rates on one invoice: switch to the itemised view. VAT is then totalled per rate, the way a VAT invoice must show it.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is the VAT calculated per line or on the invoice total?

    Both are allowed in most EU countries as long as you are consistent; the usual and most accurate way is to add up the net amounts at each rate and calculate VAT once per rate, which is the default in the itemised view. You can switch to per-line rounding if your accounting software does that.

    Why does the rounding option matter?

    VAT rarely comes out in whole cents. Rounding to the nearest cent is standard (half a cent rounds up). Some countries round cash payments to the nearest 0.05 (Switzerland, Australia and others); whole-unit rounding suits currencies like the yen and won. All sums here are done in exact arithmetic first, so there is no floating-point drift.

    What is a reverse-charge or zero-rated line?

    Zero-rated and exempt supplies carry no VAT, and under the reverse-charge mechanism the buyer accounts for the VAT instead of the seller. In all three cases the invoice shows 0% VAT, so choose the 0% rate on that line.

    How accurate are the rates?

    Standard and common reduced rates were checked against official and professional sources on the date shown above. Reduced rates apply to specific goods and services (food, books, hotels, energy ...) that differ by country, so confirm which rate applies to your product. Regional exceptions (the Canary Islands, Madeira, Azores, Corsica, Northern Ireland trade rules ...) are not modelled.
