Think of it like moving underwater

Picture someone breathing, thinking and ageing in a pool of honey. Every motion, from the heart to the molecules in their cells, is slowed together. They age less in the time you watch, but to them everything feels normal: their own seconds still last a second. Time dilation is like that, with one big difference. It is not a drag from the surroundings and there is no medium: moving fast through space simply uses up some of your motion through time, so every process, of any kind, ticks at the same slower rate when seen from home. Switch on the underwater picture to see the ship as slow-motion water. It is only an analogy: on the ship nobody feels thick air or honey.

Why must a moving clock tick slowly? Take the simplest clock there is: a light pulse bouncing between two mirrors, one tick per bounce. Light always travels at the same speed, whoever is watching. When the clock moves sideways, its light has to run a longer zig-zag path between the mirrors, so each tick takes longer.

Time dilation in plain words

Einstein's special relativity says that a clock that moves relative to you ticks more slowly than your own. The faster it moves, the bigger the effect. At everyday speeds it is far too small to notice, but it is real, it has been measured many times, and your satellite navigation has to allow for it. Close to the speed of light the effect becomes huge: at 99% of the speed of light a clock ticks about seven times slower, and at 99.999% about 224 times slower.

Does the traveller really age less?

Yes. Everything that makes time pass in a body, from the beating of the heart to the speed of chemical reactions, happens along the traveller's own path through spacetime. When they come back and meet the person who stayed home, the two really are different ages. The traveller does not feel slowed down: their watch, heartbeat and thoughts all run at the same rate, so to them nothing seems unusual. Looking out of the window, they see home's clocks running slowly, and it is the other way round when the home twin looks at them. While both move steadily, each is right about the other.

The twin paradox

If each says the other's clock is slow, who is younger at the reunion? The traveller. The two are not equal: the travelling twin has to turn round, which means accelerating and changing from one frame of reference to another, and the home twin does not. A spacetime diagram shows it neatly. A clock records the length of its own path through spacetime, and in spacetime a straight line is the longest path between two events, so the zig-zag of the traveller records less time. Is “time” just what clocks measure?

This page shows clocks slowing, but the idea runs deeper. There is no single universal time flowing in the background. What we call time is what every clock, heart and atom records along its own path, and observers moving differently disagree about how much has passed between two events, and even about which events happened “at the same time”. Many physicists see time as something that emerges from how events are connected, and the clock simulation is one way of getting hold of it. Here the rate of any ticking process is scaled by the same factor 1/γ, which is why it makes no difference whether the “clock” is a laser, a pendulum or a person.

What this simulator does not show

  • What the eye sees. The clocks here are what a careful observer works out after correcting for the time light takes to travel. A clock rushing towards you is seen running fast (a Doppler effect), then slow once it has passed. The slowdown itself is the factor γ.
  • Gravity. Clocks deeper in a gravitational field run slower too (general relativity). The calculator tables use speed only. GPS satellites have both effects; the gravity one wins, and the result is that their clocks gain about 38 microseconds a day.
  • Getting there. No object with mass can reach the speed of light, because its energy would have to be infinite. The Calculator tab shows the energy needed to reach a speed, in joules and in comparison with all the energy humanity uses in a year.

Frequently asked questions

How much would I age travelling at the speed of light?
Nothing with mass can travel at exactly the speed of light, but the closer you get the slower you age compared with home. At 99.999% of the speed of light you would age about 1 year while 224 pass at home.
Would I notice time slowing down on the ship?
No. Everything on the ship, including you, your watch and your thoughts, runs together. Only when you compare with a clock at home do you find a difference.
Does time dilation actually happen?
Yes. Muons from cosmic rays reach the ground because their clocks run slow, particle accelerators rely on it every day, and airborne atomic clocks have been compared with ones on the ground and agree with the prediction.
What is the Lorentz factor?
The number γ above, the factor by which time stretches. It is 1 at rest, 2 at 86.6% of the speed of light, 7.09 at 99% and goes to infinity as the speed gets closer to that of light.
