The electron in a hydrogen atom is not a little ball on an orbit. It is a standing wave ψnlm around the proton, and |ψ|² tells you where you are likely to find it. Every picture below is drawn from the exact solution of the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen, computed live in your browser.

What you see. A slice shows |ψ|² in one flat plane through the atom: brighter = more likely. The colour is the sign of the wave (orange +, blue −), or with complex m states its phase (the colour wheel turns m times around the z axis). The 3D cloud is a set of random “where was the electron found?” dots, drawn exactly from |ψ|². The radial view adds up all directions: P(r) = r²R² is the chance of finding the electron at distance r.

Nodes. The wave is zero on n − 1 surfaces: n − l − 1 spheres (radial nodes) and l cones or planes (angular nodes). More nodes = more wiggles = higher energy, just like a guitar string.

\( \displaystyle \psi_{n l m}(r,\theta,\varphi) = R_{n l}(r)\,Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi) \)
\( \displaystyle R_{n l}(r) = \sqrt{\left(\tfrac{2Z}{n a_0}\right)^{3}\frac{(n-l-1)!}{2n\,(n+l)!}}\;e^{-\rho/2}\rho^{\,l}\,L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(\rho) \)
\( \displaystyle \rho = \frac{2Zr}{n a_0}, \qquad \langle r\rangle = \frac{a_0}{2Z}\left[3n^2 - l(l+1)\right], \qquad P(r) = r^2 R_{nl}(r)^2 \)
L is an associated Laguerre polynomial, Y a spherical harmonic. The real orbitals (px, dxy, ...) are the sums and differences of the m and −m states that chemists draw. Spin and the small fine-structure corrections are left out.

Energy levels, jumps and the colours of hydrogen

The electron sits on one level. Click another level to make it jump: going down it sends out a photon, going up it must absorb one. The photon’s energy is the gap between the levels, so its wavelength is fixed. That is why hydrogen glows in a few sharp colours.

Air bends light a little, so a wavelength measured in air is about 0.03% shorter than in vacuum: Hα is 656.28 nm in air and 656.47 nm in vacuum. The reduced mass μ = meM/(me+M) shifts every line by about 0.05%.

Bohr’s model and the quantum cloud. Bohr (1913) pictured the electron on circular orbits of radius n²a0 and got these energies exactly right, which is why the line colours match. But it fails elsewhere: it gives the ground state angular momentum ℏ (the real value is 0: a 1s electron does not circle at all), it cannot explain line brightness or atoms with more electrons, and it says nothing about the shapes above. In the full theory the radius n²a0 survives only as the most likely radius of the orbitals with l = n − 1 (try 2p, 3d, 4f in the radial view: the peak sits exactly at 4, 9, 16 a0).
