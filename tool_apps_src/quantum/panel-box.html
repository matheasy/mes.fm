Trap a particle in a well and it can only have certain energies: a ladder of energy levels, each with its own standing wave. Pick a well, then click levels to mix them. A single level just sits there; two or more slosh back and forth in time. In the double well the particle tunnels from one side to the other and back.

Why only certain energies? A wave squeezed between walls must fit: in a box of width L only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit, so the momentum and therefore the energy come in steps, En = n²h²/(8mL²). Smaller box, bigger steps; heavier particle, smaller steps. That is why atoms (tiny boxes for light electrons) have sharp spectral lines, while a marble in a jar seems to have any energy at all.

The shape of the well sets the spacing. Infinite well: gaps grow like n². Harmonic oscillator (a spring): perfectly even steps of ℏω, starting at ½ℏω, never at zero (the zero-point energy). Morse (a real chemical bond): steps that shrink until the molecule falls apart. Finite well: only a few levels fit below the rim, and the waves poke out into the walls a little: that is tunnelling again.

Why does a mix move? Each level’s wave turns in time at its own rate, e−iEnt/ℏ. On its own that turning is invisible (|ψ|² stays put: a “stationary state”). Two levels together beat against each other, and the probability sloshes with period h/ΔE. In the double well the two lowest levels are almost equal, so the beat is slow: the particle sits in one well, then tunnels to the other and back, every h/ΔE. The ammonia maser works on exactly this flip-flop of the nitrogen atom.

How the levels are found

The well is cut into 600 to 1,200 grid points and the Schrödinger equation becomes a large matrix whose only non-zero entries are on three diagonals (the second derivative becomes (ψi−1 − 2ψi + ψi+1)/Δx²). Its eigenvalues are the energies. They are found one by one by Sturm-sequence bisection (counting how many eigenvalues lie below a trial energy, and halving the interval 40-odd times), and each wave by inverse iteration. The test suite checks the results against the exact answers: n² for the box, (n+½)ℏω for the spring, Airy-function zeros for the bouncer, the Morse formula, and the number of levels that fit in a finite well. Hard walls are placed at the ends of the picture; for wells that do not hold every level (finite, Morse), only the truly bound levels are shown.

The H₂ preset uses the usual Morse parameters for hydrogen (well depth about 4.75 eV, width parameter about 1.94 Å⁻¹, bond length 0.741 Å) and the reduced mass of two protons. The model gives a first vibrational gap near 0.52 eV, close to the measured 0.516 eV, and 17 bound levels; real H₂ has 15 (v = 0 to 14), because a real bond is not exactly a Morse curve.
