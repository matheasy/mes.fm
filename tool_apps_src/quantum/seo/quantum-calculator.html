About the quantum physics calculators

Ten small calculators for the numbers that come up again and again in quantum physics and chemistry courses. Each one updates as you type, accepts values with units (“500 nm”, “2 eV”, “1e-10 m”, “0.5c”, “25 °C”), shows the formula, your numbers plugged into it, and a one-line explanation of what the result means. A link button saves any calculation so you can share it.

What is included

  • de Broglie wavelength of an electron, proton, neutron, atom, buckyball or baseball from its speed, kinetic energy or accelerating voltage, fully relativistic (an electron through 100 V has λ = 0.1226 nm; at 100 kV relativity changes the answer by about 5%).
  • Photon energy from a wavelength, frequency, energy or wavenumber, with the colour of visible light (500 nm = 2.48 eV = 600 THz).
  • Heisenberg uncertainty: the smallest momentum and speed spread for a given position spread, and the energy spread of a short-lived state.
  • Tunnelling through a rectangular barrier: the exact transmission next to the thick-barrier and WKB approximations, with a chart against barrier width.
  • Particle in a box energy levels and the free-electron model of dye molecules (HOMO to LUMO absorption wavelength).
  • Bohr model radius, speed, energy and period for any level of hydrogen-like ions, with the reduced-mass correction.
  • Rydberg formula and a spectral line finder that names the hydrogen line closest to a wavelength.
  • Compton scattering shift (the electron Compton wavelength is 2.426 pm).
  • Blackbody radiation: Wien peak, Stefan-Boltzmann power and the Planck curve.
  • Thermal de Broglie wavelength and whether a gas is classical or quantum (nλ3).

Accuracy

The constants are the CODATA 2018 values; h, e, c and kB are exact in SI. The tunnelling and box cards are idealized textbook models (a perfectly flat barrier, infinitely high walls), so real devices and molecules differ: the cards say so where it matters.

Questions

When do I need the relativistic de Broglie formula?

When the kinetic energy is not small compared with mc² (511 keV for an electron). Below about 1 keV the difference is under 0.1%; electron microscopes at 100-300 kV need the relativistic formula, and the calculator always uses it and shows how far off the simple one would be.

Why is a baseball’s wavelength so small?

λ = h/p and Planck’s constant is tiny (6.6 × 10−34 J·s), so anything with an everyday momentum has a wavelength around 10−34 m, far smaller than a proton. No experiment could ever detect it, which is why everyday objects behave classically.

How do I convert a wavelength in nm to eV quickly?

E (eV) ≈ 1239.84 / λ (nm). So 620 nm red light is 2.0 eV and 124 nm ultraviolet is 10 eV.

Does the uncertainty principle mean the measurement disturbs the particle?

Not only that: it is a property of waves. A wave squeezed into a small region is necessarily made of a wide range of wavelengths, and so of momenta, whatever you do to measure it.
