How the simulator works

Everything here follows Newton's laws for a rigid body. The top or gyroscope is symmetric about its spin axis, with moment of inertia I₃ about that axis and I₁ across it. Its orientation is kept as a quaternion and its angular velocity in its own axes, and both are stepped forward with the 4th-order Runge-Kutta method in tiny time steps (0.1-0.2 ms), so energy and angular momentum stay constant to better than one part in a million when there is no friction (the page checks this: see "Energy drift" in the readouts).

Euler's equations\( \displaystyle \mathbf I\,\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} + \boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega) = \boldsymbol\tau \)
Gravity torque about the pivot\( \displaystyle \boldsymbol\tau = l\,\hat{\mathbf e}_3 \times m\mathbf g, \qquad |\boldsymbol\tau| = m g l \sin\theta \)
Steady precession\( \displaystyle I_1' \cos\theta\,\Omega^2 - I_3\omega_3\,\Omega + m g l = 0, \qquad \Omega \approx \frac{m g l}{I_3\,\omega_3} \)
Nutation (fast top)\( \displaystyle \omega_n \approx \frac{I_3\,\omega_3}{I_1'}, \qquad \Delta\theta \approx \frac{2 I_1' m g l \sin\theta}{(I_3\omega_3)^2} \)
Sleeping top\( \displaystyle \omega_3^2 > \frac{4 I_1' m g l}{I_3^2} \)
Gyroscope on ice\( \displaystyle F = m\,l\,\Omega^2 \sin\theta \;<\; \mu_s (M+m) g \;\Rightarrow\; \text{the block stays put} \)

Here I₁′ = I₁ + m l² is the moment of inertia across the axis about the pivot, l the distance from the pivot (or tip) to the centre of mass, θ the tilt from vertical and ω₃ the spin.

Gyroscope on a pivot

The pivot is a fixed point. Gravity's torque is horizontal, at right angles to the axis, so it does not tip the top over: it turns the angular momentum sideways, and the axis goes round (precession). Let go from rest and the axis first drops a little, then bobs up and down while it goes round (nutation): the little loops in the "seen from above" picture. Start it at exactly the steady precession speed and there is no bobbing. Two kinds of friction can be switched on: spin friction slows the spin about the axis, and precession friction resists the axis swinging round. Both are measured relative to the stand, so when you turn the stand ("Turn the holder"), friction drags on the top's motion relative to it.

Top on a table

Here nothing holds the tip: the top stands on a round tip of radius a on a flat floor, free to slide, roll and hop (six degrees of freedom). The floor's push N is worked out at every step so the tip stays on the floor; friction at the tip is Coulomb sliding friction μN (smoothed for slip speeds below 3 mm/s), and an optional rolling resistance torque c·N·a. Without friction a top on a table never rises. With sliding friction, the tip slides sideways as it spins; that friction pushes the precession along and the top rises quickly at first, then rolls round in a circle; rolling resistance then brings it up the rest of the way until it "sleeps" upright. A tippe top (centre of mass below the centre of its ball) is turned upside down by the same friction.

Gyroscope on ice

A gyroscope sits on a pivot stand fixed to a block, and the block sits on ice. For the axis to go round, its centre of mass must move in a circle, which needs a sideways force m·l·Ω² from the stand. The ice can give the block a sideways force up to μs(M+m)g without slipping. Faster spin means slower precession Ω, so much less force (it goes as 1/spin²): above a certain spin the ice holds the block still; below it, the block slides round. On perfectly frictionless ice the block always moves, in a small circle of radius m·l/(M+m), so that the centre of mass of everything stays still, at any spin. The motion is solved exactly with Lagrange's equations for the block and the gyroscope together.

Questions

Why doesn't a spinning gyroscope fall?
Gravity's torque changes the direction of its angular momentum, not its size. A torque at right angles to the angular momentum swings it round, so the axis precesses instead of falling. It does sink a little at first (the nutation dip), and friction slowly brings it down.
What makes a spinning top stand up?
In the textbook account, friction at the tip. The round tip slides on the table as the top spins; the friction force pushes the precession along, and a gyroscope whose precession is hurried rises. A top with no friction at its tip never rises. MES's experiments (see the MES tab) point out that a gyroscope set on its foot rises too, and that less precession friction and more tip friction help.
Why does the precession speed go down when the spin goes up?
The precession rate is about m g l / L, gravity's torque divided by the angular momentum. Twice the spin means half the precession.
What is a sleeping top?
A top spinning upright so fast that it hardly moves at all. Above the sleeping speed (shown in the readouts) an upright top is stable; as friction slows it below that speed it "wakes up", wobbles and falls.
Is the simulator a proof that the textbook is right?
No. A simulator can only show what a model predicts. This one uses the standard model on purpose, so you can compare its predictions with experiments such as MES's and Owen Liang's. Real measurements (spin speed, precession rate, the ice's friction, the block's movement) are what decide.
