Bending stress and beam deflection calculator

Put a beam on its supports, add point loads, spread loads (UDL) and moments, and drag them along the beam: the support reactions, the shear-force and bending-moment diagrams, the deflected shape and the bending stress update as you move them. Pick a rectangle, round bar, pipe, box section, I-beam or channel (or type I and c from a steel table), pick the material, and the calculator checks the stress against the yield strength and the deflection against a limit such as L/360. SI and US units.

How it works

  • Reactions and diagrams. The beam is solved exactly with singularity (Macaulay) functions: the two equilibrium equations plus the deflection conditions at the supports (no deflection at a support, no slope at a fixed end). That also solves the beams statics alone cannot, the fixed-ended and propped cantilever ones.
  • Bending stress: \(\sigma = M c / I = M / S\), where M is the bending moment, c the distance from the neutral axis to the outer face and I the second moment of area. The stress is zero at the neutral axis and largest at the top and bottom faces; sagging squeezes the top and stretches the bottom.
  • Deflection: from \(E I\, y'' = M(x)\), integrated twice. The picture exaggerates the bend and says by how much.
  • Shear stress at the neutral axis: \(\tau = V Q / (I t)\), 1.5 times the average V/A for a rectangle, carried almost entirely by the web of an I-beam.

Worked examples

Steel beam, 10 kN at the middle of a 4 m span. M = PL/4 = 10 kN × 4 m ÷ 4 = 10 kN·m. For an I-section 200 mm deep with 100 × 8.5 mm flanges and a 5.6 mm web, I = 1,846 cm⁴ and c = 100 mm, so σ = 10,000,000 N·mm × 100 mm ÷ 18,456,000 mm⁴ = 54 MPa: a safety factor of 4.6 against A36's 250 MPa. The deflection is PL³/48EI = 10,000 × 4³ ÷ (48 × 200 GPa × 1.846 × 10⁻⁵ m⁴) = 3.6 mm, well under L/360 = 11.1 mm.

Same beam with a spread load of 5 kN/m. M = wL²/8 = 5 × 16 ÷ 8 = 10 kN·m (the same moment as the point load: a spread load of wL = 20 kN gives the moment of a 10 kN point load at the middle), and δ = 5wL⁴/384EI = 4.5 mm.

Cantilever, 500 N at the end of a 300 mm aluminium bar, 10 × 40 mm on edge. M = PL = 150 N·m at the wall, I = 10 × 40³ ÷ 12 = 53,333 mm⁴, σ = 150,000 × 20 ÷ 53,333 = 56 MPa, δ = PL³/3EI = 500 × 0.3³ ÷ (3 × 68.9 GPa × 5.33 × 10⁻⁸) = 1.2 mm.

Questions

Why is a plank so much stiffer on edge than flat?

I = bh³/12: the depth counts cubed. A 50 × 200 mm board on edge has I = 33.3 × 10⁶ mm⁴; laid flat it has 2.1 × 10⁶ mm⁴, 16 times less, so it sags 16 times more and is 4 times more stressed.

Where is the bending moment largest?

Where the shear force crosses zero (because V = dM/dx), or at a support or a point load. For a simply supported beam that is in the span; for a cantilever, at the wall; for a beam fixed at both ends, at the walls (hogging) under a spread load.

What does L/360 mean?

A deflection limit: the beam may sag at most its span divided by 360 (11 mm on a 4 m span). Building codes use L/360 for floors under live load so they do not feel bouncy or crack the ceiling, and L/240 or L/180 for roofs. For a cantilever use its length; some codes double it.

Is the I of a real steel beam the same as here?

Nearly. Rolled sections have rounded fillets where the web meets the flanges, which add a few % to I. Use the I and c from the steel table (choose "I know I and c") for a real W or IPE shape.

What is not included?

The beam's own weight (add it as a spread load: the Section tile shows it), plastic bending past the yield point, lateral-torsional buckling of long unbraced I-beams, local buckling of thin flanges and webs, shear deflection of deep short beams, and dynamic loads.
